# Research 02: what the sources of coordination and vocabulary control say

Ticket: `issues/02-research-sources-coordination-and-vocabulary.md`. Read 2026-10-22.
Labels: *primary* = from the source itself. *second-hand* = from a text about the source. *known / inferred / guessed* = my own interpretation. Quotes are short. A location follows every claim.

## Summary

1. Eight source texts were read. ISO 25964-1 is only partly primary: the body of the standard is paywalled, so clauses 5, 7.3 and 13.6 come from labelled second-hand texts.
2. **What gets an entry** has a different test in each source: Z39.19 "each distinct concept … a single linguistic form" with warrant; courts decide "the question actually before the Court" and take only "important" splits (Rule 10); Nygard and the repo skill record only significant decisions; Parnas and Clements record every rejected alternative.
3. **Alignment:** Schelling says writers align on what is prominent and unique, often a precedent, with no talk. Z39.19 and ISO say natural language splits concepts (synonyms, homographs), so a vocabulary must prescribe one form. Courts give predictability as the reason for precedent.
4. **Gap:** in every source a named body decides and the new item has a probation status first (Z39.19 "candidate term", Nygard "proposed", ALI "draft"). In common law the lower courts act first and the top court waits ("percolation").
5. **The opposite:** only a designated body may overrule (*Rodriguez de Quijas*; Z39.19 editor and board). Anyone may distinguish a case (*Cohens*) or enter by a non-preferred term. ALI says: depart "explicitly and explain why". Schelling: without talk, a non-focal choice is "not really 'available'".
6. **Currency:** Z39.19 keeps dated history notes, USE references and (optionally) a file of deleted terms. Nygard supersedes and never edits or reuses a number. Courts test an old rule against four factors (*Casey*).
7. **Forces:** Nygard, ALI and the Supreme Court make the reasons the unit that is recorded and reviewed. Z39.19 keeps reasons inside the term record (scope and history notes). Schelling: a "rationale" gives a focal point a defence.
8. **Six disagreements** are listed below (prescribe or follow usage; settled or right; focal point or prescription; forces inside or beside the entry; what of the past stays).
9. **Writers:** all sources assume writers who can read the record. Only Nygard and Parnas and Clements write for a reader who cannot ask the author. ISO 25964 says human minds usually choose the terms. No source tests a writer with no memory.
10. **Not answered with a location:** what any source says about a writer that shares a prior but has no memory (the map's agent case). None speaks to it. The body of ISO 25964-1 is unread. See "Questions raised".

## Schelling, ch. 3 (focal points and tacit coordination)

Section names are stable. Page numbers are the e-edition's printed numbers.

### Q1. What gets a name or an entry?
- Claim: the things that coordinate are those that are *prominent* and *unique*. "A prime characteristic of most of these 'solutions' … is some kind of prominence or conspicuousness." (Tacit Coordination, p. 75.) *Primary.*
- Claim: uniqueness beats a richer but ambiguous set. "Equally essential is some kind of uniqueness; the man and his wife cannot meet at the 'lost and found' if the store has several." (p. 75.) And: "uniqueness conveys prominence; but it may be more important that uniqueness avoids ambiguousness." (Tacit Bargaining, p. 76.) *Primary.*
- Claim: extra detail can destroy the signal. An experiment where the data were "cluttered up" gave a crude 50-50 answer: "The refined signal … was drowned out by 'noise.'" (Tacit Bargaining, p. 84.) *Primary.*
- *Inferred:* Schelling gives no rule for what to name. He gives conditions for what *can* be mutually recognised.

### Q2. What makes writers align, and what makes them diverge?
- Claim: writers align on "some focal point for each person's expectation of what the other expects him to expect to be expected to do." The clue can be "analogy, precedent, accidental arrangement, symmetry, aesthetic or geometric configuration, casuistic reasoning, and who the parties are and what they know about each other." (Tacit Coordination, p. 75.) *Primary.*
- Claim: precedent works beyond its logic. "Precedent seems to exercise an influence that greatly exceeds its logical importance or legal force." (Explicit Bargaining, p. 87.) A strike settlement "sets a 'pattern' that is followed almost by default." (p. 88.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the focal point "depends on time and place and who the people are." (p. 75.) A different group may not find the same clue. *Primary.*
- Divergence: when several candidates are equally prominent, "there is but one chance in three of meeting at a house." (Tacit Bargaining, p. 76.) *Primary.*

### Q3. What happens at a gap?
- Claim: at a gap, *any* mutually recognised key becomes the key. "Finding a key—any key that is mutually recognized as the key becomes the key—may depend on imagination more than on logic." (p. 75.) *Primary.*
- Claim: who frames the problem has power. "The 'obvious' outcome depends greatly on how the problem is formulated, on what analogies or precedents the definition of the bargaining issue calls to mind." (Explicit Bargaining, p. 89.) Mediators "precipitate agreement" by "a kind of resignation of both participants." (p. 88.) *Primary.*
- Schelling does not describe a body that names new concepts. *Inferred:* the framer, mediator or first mover plays that part, by being conspicuous.

### Q4. When may a writer choose the opposite?
- Claim: without communication a non-focal choice does not coordinate. "The choices that cannot coordinate expectations are not really 'available' without communication." (Tacit Bargaining, p. 78.) *Primary.*
- Claim: with speech the focal points "may certainly be different when speech is allowed." (Explicit Bargaining, p. 95.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the party who is worse off at the focal point often takes it "simply because he knows that the other will expect him to." (p. 78.) *Primary.*

### Q5. How does a vocabulary stay current?
Schelling does not speak to a vocabulary. One related line: focal points depend on "time and place" (p. 75), so *inferred:* a focal point can move. He names "the status quo ante" as one that lasts: "a strong attraction to the status quo ante as well as to natural boundaries" (p. 88). *Primary.*

### Q6. What place do the forces (the "why") have?
- Claim: a rationale defends a focal point against the next push. One needs "a reason for standing firmly on a position"; at the focal point "the rationale may not be strong … but at least it can defend itself with the argument 'If not here, where?'" (Explicit Bargaining, p. 91.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the outcomes that attract are those with "prominence, uniqueness, simplicity, precedent, or some rationale that makes them qualitatively differentiable from the continuum of possible alternatives." (p. 91.) *Primary.*

### What Schelling assumes about its writers
- Remember: they share a history that makes some things salient (precedent, status quo ante, "who the parties are and what they know about each other", p. 75). *Primary.*
- Talk: in Tacit Coordination and Tacit Bargaining they cannot. "Coordinating their actions … when communication is impossible." (p. 71.) In Explicit Bargaining they can, and focal points still matter: "Something is perceived by both parties when communication is absent; it must still be perceptible, though undoubtedly of lesser force, when communication is possible." (p. 93.) *Primary.*
- Share a prior: yes. The text says the clue "depends on time and place and who the people are" (p. 75). *Primary.* That the clues need a shared culture is my reading: *inferred.*
- Interests: Tacit Coordination assumes the same interest; Tacit and Explicit Bargaining assume mixed interests. *Primary.*

## US Supreme Court texts on precedent (*Payne*, *Casey*, *Cohens*, *Rodriguez de Quijas*, Rule 10, *Evans* n. 1)

### Q1. What gets a name or an entry?
- Claim: what binds is the answer to the question the court actually decided. "General expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used. If they go beyond the case, they may be respected, but ought not to control the judgment in a subsequent suit when the very point is presented for decision." (*Cohens*, 19 U.S. at 399–400, the passage on *Marbury* dicta.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the reason: "The question actually before the Court is investigated with care … but their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." (*Cohens*, same passage.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the top court takes few questions. Review is "not a matter of right, but of judicial discretion"; it is "granted only for compelling reasons." (Rule 10, opening.) The listed reasons are splits and "an important question of federal law that has not been, but should be, settled by this Court" (Rule 10(a)–(c)). *Primary.*
- Claim: errors of application stay out. "A petition … is rarely granted when the asserted error consists of erroneous factual findings or the misapplication of a properly stated rule of law." (Rule 10, last sentence.) *Primary.*

### Q2. What makes writers align, and what makes them diverge?
- Claim: precedent aligns writers. Stare decisis "promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles, fosters reliance on judicial decisions, and contributes to the actual and perceived integrity of the judicial process." (*Payne*, opinion of the Court, discussion of stare decisis.) *Primary.*
- Claim: "no judicial system could do society's work if it eyed each issue afresh in every case that raised it." (*Casey*, joint opinion, Part III.) *Primary.*
- Divergence: courts of equal rank decide the same question differently. Rule 10(a) and (b) name those cases ("a decision in conflict with the decision of another United States court of appeals on the same important matter"). *Primary.*
- Divergence in a line of cases: "It also would be undesirable for the decisions in *Wilko* and *McMahon* to continue to exist side by side." (*Rodriguez*, Part III.) *Primary.*

### Q3. What happens at a gap?
- Claim: a question first arises in lower courts. The top court may wait. "Periods of 'percolation' in, and diverse opinions from, state and federal appellate courts may yield a better informed and more enduring final pronouncement by this Court." (*Evans*, 514 U.S., Ginsburg, J., dissenting, n. 1.) And, quoting Stevens, J.: "it is a sound exercise of discretion for the Court to allow the various States to serve as laboratories in which the issue receives further study before it is addressed by this Court." (*McCray*, 461 U.S. at 963, as quoted in that note.) *Primary (the Evans text), with the McCray words second-hand.*
- Claim: it takes a real case. The Court answers "the question actually before" it (*Cohens*); a petition names "questions presented … expressed concisely in relation to the circumstances of the case" (Rule 14.1(a)). *Primary.*
- Claim: change can start below. The old view "has been steadily eroded over the years, beginning in the lower courts." (*Rodriguez*, Part II.) *Primary.*

### Q4. When may a writer choose the opposite?
- Claim: a lower court may not overrule, even if it sees the precedent as eroded. "If a precedent of this Court has direct application in a case, yet appears to rest on reasons rejected in some other line of decisions, the Court of Appeals should follow the case which directly controls, leaving to this Court the prerogative of overruling its own decisions." (*Rodriguez*, Part III, 490 U.S. at 484.) *Primary.*
- Claim: any court may narrow a precedent by its facts. In *Wilko* "[t]he Court carefully limited its holding to apply only to arbitration agreements which are made 'prior to the existence of a controversy'"; "courts uniformly have concluded that *Wilko* does not apply to … existing disputes." (*Rodriguez*, Part II, n. *.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the top court may overrule on four tests: whether the rule has proved "intolerable simply in defying practical workability"; whether it is subject to reliance "that would lend a special hardship to the consequences of overruling"; whether "related principles of law have so far developed as to have left the old rule no more than a remnant of abandoned doctrine"; whether "facts have so changed or come to be seen so differently, as to have robbed the old rule of significant application or justification." (*Casey*, joint opinion, Part III, 505 U.S. at 854–55.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the default favours the old rule. Adhering to precedent "is usually the wise policy, because in most matters it is more important that the applicable rule of law be settled than it be settled right." (*Payne*, quoting *Burnet v. Coronado Oil & Gas Co.*, 285 U.S. 393, 406 (1932) (Brandeis, J., dissenting).) *Primary.*

### Q5. How does a vocabulary stay current?
- Claim: the Court overrules in a new opinion that states the reasons and the effect on pending cases. "We overrule the decision in *Wilko*." Then it decides whether the new rule applies to the present parties. "The general rule of long standing is that the law announced in the Court's decision controls the case at bar." (*Rodriguez*, Part III.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the old rule is not edited; a later opinion replaces it. *Inferred* from *Rodriguez*, which names *Wilko* and overrules it by name.

### Q6. What place do the forces have?
- Claim: the reasons are what later courts test. "Its judgment is customarily informed by a series of prudential and pragmatic considerations designed to test the consistency of overruling a prior decision with the ideal of the rule of law, and to gauge the respective costs of reaffirming and overruling a prior case." (*Casey*, Part III.) *Primary.*
- Claim: *Rodriguez* reads *Wilko* by its stated reasons: "The Court … gave two reasons", then asks whether "these reasons justify" the rule (Part II). A precedent loses force when it fails that test: "when governing decisions are unworkable or are badly reasoned, 'this Court has never felt constrained to follow prece[dent]'." (*Payne*.) *Primary.*

### What the Supreme Court texts assume about their writers
- Remember: the writers are a set of courts that read the reported opinions. *Rodriguez* and *Casey* cite earlier opinions by name and page. *Primary (by use); the rest is inferred.*
- Talk: they talk only through opinions and orders. The sources do not allow a court to ask another court what it meant. *Inferred.*
- Share a prior: yes, a trained profession and a hierarchy that the rules state. Lower courts follow "the case which directly controls" (*Rodriguez*, Part III). *Primary.*
- Limit on attention: a court looks hard only at the question before it (*Cohens*). *Primary.*

## House of Lords, Practice Statement (Judicial Precedent), 1966

### Q1. What gets a name or an entry? 
One line: the Statement does not speak to it.

### Q2. Alignment and divergence
- Claim: precedent gives certainty. "Their Lordships regard the use of precedent as an indispensable foundation upon which to decide what is the law … It provides at least some degree of certainty upon which individuals can rely in the conduct of their affairs, as well as a basis for orderly development of legal rules." (Statement, para. 1.) *Primary.*

### Q3. A gap
One line: the Statement does not speak to it.

### Q4. When may a writer choose the opposite?
- Claim: the House may depart from its own earlier decision "when it appears right to do so", treating "former decisions of this House as normally binding." (para. 2.) It names reasons for care: "the danger of disturbing retrospectively the basis on which contracts, settlements of property and fiscal arrangements have been entered into and also the especial need for certainty as to the criminal law." (para. 3.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the rule binds only that House. "This announcement is not intended to affect the use of precedent elsewhere than in this House." (para. 4.) *Primary.*

### Q5. Currency
- Claim: "too rigid adherence to precedent may lead to injustice in a particular case and also unduly restrict the proper development of the law." (para. 2.) *Primary.*

### Q6. Place of the forces
One line: it names forces for and against change (certainty, reliance, injustice, development) but does not say they must be recorded. *Primary; the last half is inferred.*

### What the Statement assumes about its writers
The writers are the Lords themselves. They remember their own earlier decisions (they are asked to depart from them) and they can speak to each other. The Statement is made "on behalf of the Lords of Appeal in Ordinary and myself" (Hansard, opening). *Primary.* Share a prior: a court of one profession. *Inferred.*

## American Law Institute: Restatements (Director's letter, FAQ, Style Manual)

### Q1. What gets a name or an entry?
- Claim: a Restatement states a rule when it passes four tests. "The first is to ascertain the nature of the majority rule … The second step is to ascertain trends in the law … A third step is to determine what specific rule fits best with the broader body of law and therefore leads to more coherence in the law. And the fourth step is to ascertain the relative desirability of competing rules." (Style Manual, p. 5. The letter's source note 12 cites the same manual for the "declines to follow" rule on p. 6.) *Primary.*
- Claim: ALI may give a name to something that has none. A Restatement may be one that "distills a rule that is latent in a body of case law, but which never has been expressly announced by a court", or one that "offers new terminology to describe what courts are doing." (Director's letter, paragraph beginning "This transparency".) *Primary.*

### Q2. What makes writers align, and what makes them diverge?
- Claim: the stated reason for the Institute's existence is uncertainty and complexity in the law. The 1923 report says the object is "to help make certain much that is now uncertain and to simplify unnecessary complexities, but also to promote those changes which will tend better to adapt the laws to the needs of life." (Director's letter, ¶2, quoting the 1923 Report at 14.) *Primary for the letter; second-hand for the Report.*
- Claim: word choice itself aligns readers. "Conceptual clarity calls for repetition of the same terms when the same thing is meant. Use of a different term, even one that is nearly synonymous, may suggest to the reader that a different meaning is intended." (Style Manual, "Language and Structure", Diction, 1.e "Be consistent in terminology", pp. 22–23.) *Primary.*
- Divergence: states that disagree. The letter says a Restatement of a minority rule is "the law that 'is'" in the minority jurisdiction, and a recommendation to change in the others. (Director's letter, ¶ beginning "Moreover".) *Primary.*

### Q3. What happens at a gap?
- Claim: Reporters draft; others review. "Reporters structure the project, prepare drafts, and present drafts to Advisers and Members Consultative Group (MCG) participants for discussion." The final text "will represent the considered scholarship, experience, and judgment of the ALI as a whole." (FAQ, ¶1.) *Primary.*
- Claim: ALI is explicit that a choice at a gap is a judgment. "These are matters of judgment, similar to the kind of judgment exercised by a common-law judge." (Director's letter, "This transparency" paragraph.) *Primary.*
- Claim: where courts split, "a choice had to be made and naturally we chose the view we thought was right." (Goodrich, 1948, as quoted in the letter ¶3.) *Second-hand quote inside a primary letter.*

### Q4. When may a writer choose the opposite?
- Claim: "if a Restatement declines to follow the majority rule, it should say so explicitly and explain why." (Style Manual, p. 6; also quoted in the Director's letter, ¶5.) *Primary.*
- Claim: terms: "Departures from standard, well-established terminology therefore need not be avoided but should be carefully considered … Changes of this sort and their legal or stylistic objectives should be expressly noted." (Style Manual, I.B.1.c(i), p. 8.) *Primary.*
- Claim: a Reporter is "not bound to adhere to particular verbal formulations found even in the opinions that support the proposition asserted." (Style Manual, p. 8.) *Primary.*

### Q5. How does a vocabulary stay current?
- Claim: ALI revises by series: the letter names "the Restatement First series … completed between 1923 and 1944" and later "Restatement of the Law, Second" and "Third". (Director's letter, ¶3–4.) *Primary.* *Inferred:* a Restatement is replaced by a later series; it is not edited in place.
- Claim: the stated aim is clarity "in its own work": the Institute "must doggedly pursue such clarity" (letter, ¶5). *Primary.*

### Q6. What place do the forces have?
- Claim: reasons are part of the text. "If a minority rule is chosen, the rationale for selecting it over the majority rule should be clearly articulated." (Director's letter, ¶ beginning "Moreover".) And the Style Manual: "if a Restatement declines to follow the majority rule, it should say so explicitly and explain why" (p. 6). *Primary.*
- Claim: the working formula: "we should feel obliged in our deliberations to give weight to all of the considerations that the courts, under a proper view of the judicial function, deem it right to weigh in theirs." (Wechsler, as quoted in the letter ¶4.) *Second-hand quote inside a primary letter.*

### What ALI assumes about its writers
- Writers of a Restatement (Reporters, Advisers, MCG, Council) talk, argue and vote: "oftentimes no participant—even our Reporters—walks away from the process fully in agreement with every section of the publication." (FAQ, ¶1.) *Primary.*
- The readers (courts, lawyers) read the text and are free to follow or not. Restatements "were meant … to be at once analytical, critical and constructive" (1923 Report, via letter ¶2), and a Wechsler formula says a majority "would not be thought to be conclusive" (letter ¶4). *Second-hand for the quotes.*
- Share a prior: the law profession. *Inferred.*

## ANSI/NISO Z39.19-2005 (R2010)

### Q1. What gets a name or an entry?
- Claim: each distinct concept gets one form. "A major goal of vocabulary control is to ensure that each distinct concept is described by a single linguistic form." (§5.3, Principles.) *Primary.*
- Claim: warrant decides what is included and what form it takes. Three warrants: "the natural language used to describe content objects (literary warrant), the language of users (user warrant), and the needs and priorities of the organization (organizational warrant)." (§5.3.5.) *Primary.*
- Claim: scope is limited. "The scope of terms is restricted to selected meanings within the domain of the controlled vocabulary." (§6.2.) *Primary.*
- Claim: detail at the edge stays out. "The addition of highly specific terms is usually restricted to the core area of the subject field … because the proliferation of such terms in fringe areas is likely to lead to a controlled vocabulary that is difficult to manage." (§11.1.7, p. 94.) *Primary.*
- Claim: unused terms can enter to complete a structure: terms "that have not yet been used in indexing are frequently admitted … on the grounds that they are necessary to complete a hierarchy" (§11.1.8). Very rarely or very often assigned terms are "candidates for modification or deletion" (§11.3.1.3). *Primary.*
- Claim: the one-of-a-kind also gets entries. "Unique entities, or 'classes-of-one,' are usually expressed as proper nouns." (§6.3.3.) *Primary.*
- Claim: do not start from nothing. "Before embarking on the substantial work of creating a new controlled vocabulary, the sponsoring organization should ascertain whether an existing controlled vocabulary covers the same or an overlapping domain." (§11.1.1.) *Primary.*

### Q2. What makes writers align, and what makes them diverge?
- Claim: divergence comes from two features of language. "Two or more words or terms can be used to represent a single concept"; "Two or more words that have the same spelling can represent different concepts." (§1.1.) *Primary.*
- Claim: alignment comes from three methods: "Defining the scope, or meaning, of terms; Using the equivalence relationship to link synonymous and nearly synonymous terms; and Distinguishing among homographs." (§1.2.) *Primary.*
- Claim: one preferred term per concept; the others point to it. "The vocabulary should list the other synonyms and variants as non-preferred terms with USE references to the preferred term." (§5.3.2; also §8.2.) *Primary.*
- Claim: alignment can be tested. "Indexing experiments where several people index the same items can be very useful; disagreements can point out problems in the controlled vocabulary." (§11.2.2, p. 96.) *Primary.*

### Q3. What happens at a gap?
- Claim: the user of the vocabulary nominates; an editor and board decide. "Whenever an appropriate term or combination of terms cannot be found in a controlled vocabulary, the indexer or searcher should nominate a new term as a candidate term … reviewed by the editor of the controlled vocabulary, and preferably by an editorial board." (§11.3.1.1, p. 96.) *Primary.*
- Claim: four decisions per nominated term: add or covered by an existing term (then maybe a USE reference); scope notes; correct form; relation to existing terms. (§11.3.1.1 (a)–(d).) *Primary.*
- Claim: a candidate is marked and often hidden from users. "Candidate terms … are proposed terms that have not gone through all acceptance procedures. These terms should be marked by a special symbol or phrase … In an online system in which the controlled vocabulary is linked to a single database, candidate terms are generally not displayed to users." (§11.1.6, p. 93.) *Primary.*
- Claim: before admission a term is checked against "technical dictionaries, glossaries, scholarly monographs … existing controlled vocabularies; and classification schemes." (§11.1.5.) *Primary.*

### Q4. When may a writer choose the opposite?
- Claim: an individual user may use a non-preferred term and be redirected. "A cross-reference to the preferred term should be made from any 'equivalent' entry term." (§8.2.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the standard allows listed exceptions to its own rules when warrant supports them: overlapping meanings and homographs "should be avoided as far as possible", but "literary warrant … and other criteria may, however, justify the use of such terms" (§6.2); singular count nouns are acceptable "if … there is literary or user warrant" (§6.5.1.1); a synonym ring is "an exception" to the one-preferred-term rule (§5.3.2, note). *Primary.*
- Claim: a change to an existing term goes through a proposal with a reason. "Indexers and searchers should be able to propose modifications to existing terms or their relationships, explaining the rationale and supplying supporting documentation for the proposed changes." (§11.3.1.2.) *Primary.*

### Q5. How does a vocabulary stay current?
- Claim: "because language changes over time, a controlled vocabulary is not frozen in time — new concepts emerge, terminology and usage change, and some terms go out of fashion or become obsolete." (§2.5.) "Policies and procedures should be established for periodic review of terminology, establishment of new terms, and replacement of obsolete terms." (§11.3.1, p. 96.) *Primary.*
- Claim: every change is dated and owned. "The term record should note the date of each change and identify the individual responsible for it." (§11.3, p. 96.) *Primary.*
- Claim: on change, the old form stays reachable. "If a term is modified, the date of the change should be recorded in the history note … and a USE reference should be made from the old form to the new form." (§11.3.1.2, p. 97.) *Primary.*
- Claim: a retired term may stay, marked. "A deleted or modified term may be retained in the controlled vocabulary for retrieval or historical purposes only. If it is retained, it must be marked, e.g., 'for retrieval purposes only'." (§11.3.2.1(a), p. 97.) Or it may be removed: "If it is discovered, however, that a term has been established and assigned in error … its record may be deleted from the controlled vocabulary as well." (§11.3.2.1(c).) *Primary.*
- Claim: deleted terms may be kept in a separate file: "retaining a record of their scope and history in order to track such decisions in case a term from this group is later reconsidered." (§11.3.2.2, p. 97.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the cost of a change falls on past records. "Whenever a term is modified or deleted … the impact of the change on the ability to search previously indexed database records must be considered." (§11.3.2.1, p. 97.) *Primary.*

### Q6. What place do the forces have?
- Claim: the scope note carries the chosen meaning and the excluded ones. "A scope note should state the chosen meaning of a term. It may also indicate other meanings that are recognized in natural language, but which have been deliberately excluded." (§6.2.2, p. 22.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the history note carries the reasons for change. "The note should also track the history of modifications …, recording previous forms with reasons for the change." (§11.3.2.2.) And §6.2.3: "It is especially important to indicate when and how a term has changed over time." *Primary.*
- Claim: warrant is the justification for an entry. "literary warrant: Justification for the representation of a concept in an indexing language or for the selection of a preferred term because of its frequent occurrence in the literature." (§4.1 Definitions.) *Primary.*
- Claim: "Changes in the scope of terms should be recorded in History Notes." (§6.2.2.) *Primary.*

### What Z39.19 assumes about its writers
- Roles: compilers, an editor and an editorial board, indexers (who describe content with the terms) and searchers (who look up content). (§11.3.1.1; §2.6: "both those who employ it to describe content and those who use it to locate needed information".) *Primary.*
- Remember: indexers and searchers do not have to. The vocabulary carries the meaning: "Each term should be formulated in such a way that it conveys the intended scope to any user of the controlled vocabulary." (§6.2.) *Primary.*
- Talk: the writers (indexers) do not talk to each other in the model. They talk to the editor through nominations and forms (§11.3.1.1; Appendix E forms). *Primary for the channel; inferred that no other channel is assumed.*
- Share a prior: partly. Terms are to "match as closely as possible the prevailing usage in the domain's literature" (§5.3.5.1) and "should reflect the usage of people familiar with the domain" (§6.6.1). *Primary.* That the writers are assumed to share that domain is *inferred.*
- Humans: the text speaks of indexers, searchers and editors as people. *Inferred.* The ISO standard states it outright (see below).

## ISO 25964-1:2011 (with second-hand texts for the paywalled body)

### Q1. What gets a name or an entry?
- Claim: the unit is the *concept*, not the word. "2.11 concept: unit of thought. NOTE Concepts can often be expressed in a variety of different ways. They exist in the mind as abstract entities independent of terms used to express them." (ISO 25964-1, §2.11, p. 3.) *Primary.*
- Claim: a controlled vocabulary is a "prescribed list of terms, headings or codes, each representing a concept … designed for applications in which it is useful to identify each concept with one consistent label." (§2.12, p. 3.) *Primary.*
- Claim: ISO gives rules on whether to admit a complex concept (§7.3, p. 39, title only). Content *not read*. A summary says the editors "should decide whether a complex idea should remain as one term or be split into simpler constituent concepts", "That decision affects recall and precision." (iteh.ai catalogue summary, "Handling complex concepts"; *second-hand*. It says "Clause 8"; the 2011 table of contents has complex concepts in clause 7. The summary may describe edition 2. I do not know.)
- Claim: ELSST (applying ISO) admits a concept if it "does not overlap with an existing concept to such an extent that it cannot be clearly distinguished from it" and "is not culture-, language- or institution specific", and if it has data, is a useful emerging concept, or is a "bucket" concept for structure. It warns that an emerging concept "may be terms used by only one researcher which do not gain currency in the literature." (ELSST guidelines §14.3.1, pp. 25–26.) *Second-hand.*

### Q2. What makes writers align, and what makes them diverge?
- Claim: the main principle is shared choice. "If both the indexer and the searcher are guided to choose the same term for the same concept, then relevant documents will be retrieved. This is the main principle underlying thesaurus design." (Introduction, p. vi.) *Primary.*
- Claim: a concept is more than a label. "concept = preferred term + any non-preferred terms + any scope note + any clues given by relationships to broader terms and others … It all seems to rely on human interpretation - the weakness and the strength of the thesaurus model." (Dextre Clarke email, 2004-03-17.) *Second-hand.*
- Divergence: not stated in the preview beyond the equivalence rules (entry terms, §2.16: "not for direct use in metadata, but for the purpose of guiding the user to another term"). *Primary.*

### Q3. What happens at a gap?
- Claim: ELSST takes suggestions "from both ELSST developers and end-users on a continuous basis … and reviewed at regular intervals." (ELSST §14.1, p. 25.) *Second-hand.*
- Claim: ISO clause 13.6.1 says something about making a newly introduced term available. The snippet is cut off ("ele deve ser disponibilizado para pes…"); that it means "for searching" is *guessed*. I saw only a Portuguese quotation of the standard in a thesis snippet. (My translation: "it is essential to foresee changes, both in the natural evolution of terminology and in the scope of the databases or other resources the thesaurus serves", p. 96.) *Second-hand of second-hand; weak.*

### Q4. When may a writer choose the opposite?
One line: not in the parts read.

### Q5. How does a vocabulary stay current?
- Claim: ISO's scope is "development and maintenance of thesauri" (§1, p. 1), and clause 13 is "Managing thesaurus construction and maintenance" with "13.6 Updating" at p. 95 (table of contents only). *Primary, title only.*
- Claim: "Thesaurus maintenance must be carried out on a continual basis … Changes to concepts, terms and structure need to be carefully managed in order to preserve the integrity of the thesaurus and the data it is used to index. All changes to the thesaurus must be recorded, and any records that are dependent on those changes, corrected." (ELSST §14.1, p. 25.) *Second-hand.*
- Claim: "Where data descriptions have not been updated … retaining the old Preferred Term as a Use For term enables users to find the data using the old Preferred Term." (ELSST §16.4.2, p. 34.) *Second-hand.*

### Q6. What place do the forces have?
- Claim: the standard keeps the concept apart from the word and lets scope notes, relations and entry terms define it together (Dextre Clarke, above). §5.2 "Scope notes" and §5.3 "Reciprocal scope notes" exist (pp. 20–21, table of contents only). *Primary (titles); second-hand for the idea.*
- ELSST gives a source for each scope note, in a ranked order, ending with "writing the Scope Note from scratch". (ELSST §14.3.5, p. 27.) *Second-hand.*

### What ISO 25964 assumes about its writers
- Humans choose: "this part of ISO 25964 retains the assumption that human intellect is usually involved in the selection of indexing terms and in the selection of search terms … even though a thesaurus may also be applied in situations where computers make the choices." (Introduction, p. vi.) *Primary.*
- Users: the Introduction says thesauri used to be "designed for information professionals trained in indexing and searching", and now "there is a demand for vocabularies that untrained users will find to be intuitive." (p. vi.) *Primary.*
- Remember / talk: not stated in the parts read. *Inferred:* as in Z39.19, the writers talk to editors, not to each other.

## Nygard, "Documenting Architecture Decisions" (2011)

### Q1. What gets a name or an entry?
- Claim: "We will keep a collection of records for 'architecturally significant' decisions: those that affect the structure, non-functional characteristics, dependencies, interfaces, or construction techniques." (Decision.) *Primary.*
- Claim: one record, one decision. "One ADR describes one significant decision for a specific project. It should be something that has an effect on how the rest of the project will run." (Consequences.) *Primary.*
- Claim: small beats large. "Large documents are never kept up to date. Small, modular documents have at least a chance at being updated." (Context.) *Primary.*

### Q2. What makes writers align, and what makes them diverge?
- Claim: a new person without the motive has "only two choices": "Blindly accept the decision" or "Blindly change it." (Context.) *Primary.*
- Claim: both fail. "If the project accumulates too many decisions accepted without understanding, then the development team becomes afraid to change anything and the project collapses under its own weight." And a blind change "could mean damaging the project's overall value without realizing it." (Context.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the cure is shared motive. "The motivation behind previous decisions is visible for everyone, present and future." (Consequences.) *Primary.*

### Q3. What happens at a gap?
- Claim: a new decision has a status before it is shared. "A decision may be 'proposed' if the project stakeholders haven't agreed with it yet, or 'accepted' once it is agreed." (Decision, Status.) *Primary.*
- Claim: decisions build on each other. "The consequences of one ADR are very likely to become the context for subsequent ADRs … the large-scale responses create spaces for the smaller scale to fit into." (Consequences.) *Primary.*
- The post does not say who writes or who agrees beyond "project stakeholders". *Primary.*

### Q4. When may a writer choose the opposite?
- Claim: reversal is allowed and recorded. "If a decision is reversed, we will keep the old one around, but mark it as superseded. (It's still relevant to know that it *was* the decision, but is *no longer* the decision.)" (Decision.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the stated worry is a reversal made without the context ("Blindly change it", Context). *Primary.*

### Q5. How does a vocabulary stay current?
- Claim: "ADRs will be numbered sequentially and monotonically. Numbers will not be reused." A superseded or deprecated ADR carries "a reference to its replacement." (Decision, Status.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the time to revisit "will be clear from changes in the project's context." (Consequences.) *Primary.*
- Nygard does not speak of a *vocabulary*, only of decision records. *Inferred.*

### Q6. What place do the forces have?
- Claim: forces are the content. "Each record describes a set of forces and a single decision in response to those forces. Note that the decision is the central piece here, so specific forces may appear in multiple ADRs." (Decision.) *Primary.*
- Claim: "Context … describes the forces at play, including technological, political, social, and project local. These forces are probably in tension, and should be called out as such. The language in this section is value-neutral." (Decision, Context.) *Primary.*
- Claim: the form is borrowed. An ADR is "a short text file in a format similar to an Alexandrian pattern. (Though the decisions themselves are not necessarily patterns, they share the characteristic balancing of forces.)" (Decision.) *Primary.*
- Claim: consequences list "all of them … not just the 'positive' ones." (Decision, Consequences.) *Primary.*

### What Nygard assumes about its writers
- Remember: no. The reader is "a new person coming on to a project" and "team composition changes over time". (Context; Consequences.) *Primary.*
- Talk: the reader cannot ask the author. Each ADR is written "as if it is a conversation with a future developer." (Decision.) *Primary for the quote; the "cannot ask" part is inferred from "as if".*
- Share a prior: developers on one project; a repo, with Markdown, in version control. (Decision; Experience Report.) *Primary.*
- Evidence: a self-report only. "[S]ix to ten developers rotate through projects using ADRs. All of them have stated that they appreciate the degree of context they received by reading them." (Experience Report.) *Primary; self-reported and not measured.*

## Where the sources disagree

1. **Should a vocabulary follow usage or lead it?**
   - Follow: Z39.19 §6.6.1.1: "Words and phrases drawn from the literature of the field should determine the formulation of terms. When two or more variants have literary warrant, the most frequently used term should be selected as the term."
   - Lead: ALI Style Manual, p. 8: Reporters "are not bound to adhere to particular verbal formulations found even in the opinions"; "Clarifying, and if necessary improving, established but insufficiently examined formulas has been an objective of the Institute from the beginning."
   - Note: both require a reason. Z39.19 §11.3.1.2 asks for "rationale and … supporting documentation"; ALI asks for "explain why" (p. 6). *Inferred.*

2. **When does being settled outweigh being right?**
   - Settled: *Payne* quoting Brandeis: "in most matters it is more important that the applicable rule of law be settled than it be settled right."
   - Not settled: Practice Statement para. 2: "too rigid adherence to precedent may lead to injustice … and also unduly restrict the proper development of the law." And Nygard, Context: decisions "accepted without understanding" leave the team "afraid to change anything".
   - Positions in between: *Casey*'s four tests (Part III).

3. **Does alignment come from prominence or from prescription?**
   - Prominence: Schelling, p. 75: coordination works with no talk by "analogy, precedent, accidental arrangement, symmetry".
   - Prescription: Z39.19 §1.1–1.2 and ISO 25964-1 §2.12: natural language gives synonyms and homographs, so a list that is "prescribed" must say which form to use.
   - *Inferred:* Z39.19 treats unaided prominence as unreliable in a domain with many writers; Schelling treats prominence as reliable only if the clue is unique and shared.

4. **Do the reasons live inside the entry or beside it?**
   - Inside: Z39.19 keeps scope note, history note and "reasons for the change" in the term record (§6.2.2, §11.3.2.2).
   - Beside: Nygard and the repo skill keep the decision and its forces in a separate record, and the skill keeps its glossary "devoid of implementation details" (Nygard, Decision; SKILL.md, "Update CONTEXT.md inline"). ISO's concept equation puts the scope note inside the entry (Dextre Clarke email).

5. **What of the past stays visible?**
   - Stays: Nygard: "keep the old one around, but mark it as superseded"; numbers "will not be reused". Z39.19 §11.3.2.1(a) and §11.3.2.2: retire with a history note; optionally keep a "separate file of deleted terms".
   - May go: Z39.19 §11.3.2.1(c): a term assigned in error may have its record deleted. Parnas and Clements, §VII: documentation for readers who "want to understand the programs, not to relive their discovery", yet they record rejected alternatives.

6. **What earns a record?**
   - Few: Nygard ("architecturally significant"); SKILL.md ("If any of the three is missing, skip the ADR"); Z39.19 §11.1.7 (keep fringe detail out).
   - All alternatives: Parnas and Clements §VII: "all of the design alternatives that we considered and rejected".

## Questions raised

1. Every source that handles a gap uses a probation state (candidate, proposed, draft) and a named decider. Does a writer with no memory ever read the probation items, and what does it do with them? (Z39.19 §11.1.6 hides candidates from users; Nygard Status shows them.)
2. The sources with a hierarchy (courts) separate who may *distinguish* from who may *overrule*. Which of those two powers does a writer have when it cannot ask anyone? (*Rodriguez*, Part III; *Cohens*.)
3. Z39.19 tests alignment by letting several people index the same items (§11.2.2). What is the matching test for several writers who write the same piece?
4. Schelling's answer depends on a shared culture ("who the people are", p. 75). What is the shared culture of writers that share a prior but no memory? Which of Schelling's clues (precedent, uniqueness, symmetry) remain for them? *Guessed:* the text and the written record, since memory is absent.
5. ISO 25964 states that "human intellect" usually picks the terms (Introduction, p. vi). Do any of these sources say what changes when a machine picks them? Only that one clause speaks to it.
6. Common law and ALI both write the reason into the text. Z39.19 writes it in notes. Nygard writes it as the content. Is there a single place the reasons must live, or does it depend on who the readers are?
7. Which of the six "what earns a record" tests (Z39.19 warrant, the court's "important question", Nygard's "significant", the skill's three parts, ELSST's overlap and currency tests, Parnas's "all alternatives") is a test of the *concept*, and which is a test of the *decision*? *Inferred:* Z39.19 and ISO test concepts; Nygard, the skill and Parnas test decisions; courts and ALI test both.
8. Z39.19 weighs the cost of a change by its effect on *earlier records* (§11.3.2.1). What are the earlier records for a codebase, and who re-indexes them?
9. For ISO 25964 clauses 5, 7.3 and 13.6 the primary text is not read. Do they add a rule on adding, scoping or removing a concept that changes any claim above? I do not know.
10. The map asks about a team of people and agents across several teams. None of these sources covers a vocabulary shared across bodies with separate editors, other than Z39.19 §11.1.1 and §11.1.7 (link specialised vocabularies to a general one) and ELSST's shared "core" concepts (guidelines p. 9).
